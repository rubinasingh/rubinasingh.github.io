Transportation decarbonization depends not only on new technologies and infrastructure, but also on how people respond to them. Policies can subsidize cleaner technologies, but emissions reductions depend on whether and how people use them.

My research addresses this gap through three connected themes: policy design, causal evaluation, and behavioral mechanisms.

I aim to build a research program that uses behavioral evidence to design, evaluate, and improve transportation decarbonization policies.

Research

My research addresses this gap through three connected themes: policy design, causal evaluation, and behavioral mechanisms. My work on e-bike incentive programs illustrates this framework.

01

Policy Design

Designing effective transportation incentives requires understanding how the size of the incentive influences adoption. However, rebate amounts are often determined with limited empirical evidence on their behavioral effects or cost-effectiveness. My current research exploits differences in rebate size across two Washington State e-bike rebate programs to estimate how purchase rates respond to incentive amount, providing evidence on the rebate level that most effectively induces adoption per public dollar.

02

Causal Evaluation

In work presented at the 2026 TRB Annual Meeting and currently under review, I designed and fielded a randomized controlled trial with passively collected GPS data to estimate the causal effect of e-bike rebates on travel behavior. Although rebates increased e-bike ownership, they produced no detectable reduction in vehicle miles traveled or associated emissions. I am now applying a similar identification strategy to an e-bike lending library, combining randomized assignment with passively collected trip data to test whether temporary access produces the travel changes that ownership subsidies did not.

03

Behavioral Mechanisms

Average treatment effects show whether a policy works, but not why or for whom. I am extending this work using causal machine learning to estimate how responses to e-bike incentives vary across household characteristics, the built environment, and baseline travel patterns. Identifying when and for whom e-bikes substitute for car trips will help agencies target programs where they are most effective.

Broader portfolio

My work on public charging reliability, published in Transport Policy, showed that perceived charging reliability influences electric vehicle adoption, highlighting the role of infrastructure quality alongside traditional factors such as cost and range. My work on micromobility mode choice examines how latent behavioral factors shape choices among shared e-scooters and e-bikes. In ongoing work with the Port of Seattle, I evaluate how charging infrastructure and policy design can encourage transportation network company drivers to adopt electric vehicles.

Future research will extend my current work from evaluating individual programs to designing coordinated policy portfolios. Using linked administrative records and passively collected travel data, I will estimate how these programs interact within households and identify how limited public funds can be allocated to maximize emissions reductions per dollar.
